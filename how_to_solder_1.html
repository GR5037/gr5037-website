How to Solder

A beginner's guide to safe and reliable soldering.

← Back to Learning Center
Beginner Estimated time: 30–45 minutes

Introduction

Soldering joins metal surfaces using a filler metal called solder. It is a useful skill for electronics, wiring, and robotics projects.

In this guide, you will learn about the equipment, safety practices, and basic techniques needed to create reliable solder joints.

Tools & Materials

Gather the following items before you begin.

  • Soldering iron or temperature-controlled station
  • Suitable solder for the project
  • Soldering iron stand and tip cleaner
  • Safety glasses
  • Fume extractor or suitable local ventilation
  • Wire and practice materials
  • Wire strippers, if working with wires

Safety First

Before Turning on the Iron

  • Wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from accidental splashes and clipped wire ends.
  • Use a fume extractor near the soldering point and avoid breathing soldering fumes.
  • Keep the hot iron in its stand whenever you are not actively using it.
  • Never touch the iron tip or freshly soldered joints; both can cause burns.
  • Wash your hands after handling solder, especially if it contains lead. Never eat or drink at the workbench.
  • Keep the work area clear of flammable materials and follow your team's workshop rules.

Basic Soldering Technique

Practice on scrap wire or a training board before soldering anything on your robot.

01

Prepare Your Materials

Make sure the parts are clean, stable, and positioned so they will not move during soldering. If you are joining wires, strip only the amount of insulation needed and keep the exposed conductors neatly arranged.

02

Heat the Connection

Place the iron tip so it contacts both surfaces you want to join. The goal is to heat the connection itself so solder can flow onto the metal surfaces.

Do not rely on melting solder against the iron tip alone. A properly heated joint allows the solder to flow where it is needed.

03

Apply the Solder

Feed a small amount of solder to the heated connection, rather than piling solder onto the iron tip. Use enough to wet the joint without creating an excessive blob.

Remove the solder first, then remove the iron. Allow the joint to cool without moving the parts.

04

Inspect Your Work

Check that the solder has wetted the intended surfaces and that the connection is secure. Look for loose wires, unintended bridges between adjacent contacts, or excess solder.

If the joint is poor, let it cool and rework it safely. Do not pull on a joint while the solder is still molten.

Common Mistakes

Cold Solder Joint

A joint that did not heat properly may have poor wetting or an unreliable connection. Reheat the connection correctly and allow it to cool without movement.

Too Much Solder

Excess solder can hide the connection or bridge nearby contacts. Apply only enough solder to form a suitable joint.

Moving the Joint Too Soon

Movement while solder solidifies can weaken the joint. Keep the parts still until the solder has cooled.

Poor Fume Control

Soldering fumes should not be breathed in. Use a fume extractor positioned near the work and follow workshop ventilation rules.

Final Checklist

  • The solder wets the intended metal surfaces.
  • The connection is mechanically stable.
  • No unintended solder bridges are present.
  • Wires and nearby components are undamaged.
  • The iron is safely returned to its stand.
  • The work area is clean and safe.

Putting It Into Practice

Start by practicing on scrap wire or a training board. Focus on heating the connection properly, applying the right amount of solder, and inspecting each joint before moving on.

Ask a mentor to review your work before soldering electrical connections on a robot.
